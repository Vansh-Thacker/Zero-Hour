The challenge

ScottishPower's Cyber team has a tabletop game. It is used at graduate onboarding events and in some team training sessions. Players run a fictional energy business, spend a budget on cyber defences, and watch a realistic attack play out.

Alongside it, the team has a set of questions about everyday cyber behaviour, such as what to do with a suspicious email. Today these are a separate quiz.

We think there is a much bigger digital opportunity. Your job is to turn the tabletop game into a digital game that is engaging, accessible and ready to scale, with the questions built into it.

You do not need any cyber security knowledge. All the cyber content is written by our Cyber team and supplied in this pack. You bring the software and the game design.

Your players are colleagues learning together, often around one screen with a facilitator (the person running the session). The game should still explain itself clearly without one.

Name your game. The digital game does not have a name yet, so give yours a name of your own. Name the fictional business too. Set each name in one place in your code or data, so that it can be changed later without searching for it.

The attack, hour by hour

Players choose their defences once, before the attack starts. Then the attack plays out in four phases, from late on Monday afternoon to just before the day shift on Tuesday. After each phase, players see how their choices performed and answer a few behaviour questions.

Rules of the core game

Players choose controls. A control is a cyber defence, such as email security or a backup service. There are 18 to choose from, in three categories: Prevent Detect Respond. Each has a price, and each scores different points in each phase.

  1. Choose up front. The business has a budget of £3 million for controls. Players choose all their controls before the attack starts, setting up their business to be as secure as they can. They can spend up to £3 million. They do not have to spend it all.
  2. Cover all three categories. Players must choose at least one Prevent, one Detect and one Respond control. They can choose as many more as they can afford. Each control can be chosen once.
  3. Confirm. Players can change their choice until they confirm it. After that it is fixed for the whole game.
  4. Already in place. Three controls are already in place in the business: a firewall, vulnerability scanning and penetration testing. Show them to players. They cannot be chosen, they score nothing, and they do not count towards rule 2.
  5. The attack unfolds. The game plays the four phases in order. For each phase, show the story, then reveal how many points each chosen control scores in that phase. Every chosen control scores in every phase.
  6. Behaviour checkpoint. At the end of each phase, players answer behaviour questions. A secure answer earns extra points. See "Foundation: the behaviour questions" below.
  7. Final outcome. After phase 4, show the control score, the behaviour points and the two added together, with a debrief explaining what happened and why. A reset starts a new game.

The supplied scoring module checks the budget, the three categories and the choose-once rule, and works out the control score for each phase. Confirming, the story, revealing each phase, the checkpoints, the final outcome and reset are yours to build.

This is a teaching game. The controls, prices and points are simplified for learning, not advice on what to buy. The business, its systems and its people are fictional.

Foundation: the behaviour questions

The controls game teaches how an organisation defends itself. People also need to know how to behave safely as individuals. Our Cyber team has written 40 questions for this. Each has four options, one correct answer and an explanation.

  • There are 10 topics, such as phishing, passwords and physical security.
  • Each topic has one question at each of four tiers of difficulty: Spot the Risk, Read the Signs, Choose Your Response and Pressure Test.
  • 16 questions match a phase of the attack story. The other 24 are general and can be used at any checkpoint.

They are all in 1-foundation/data/questions.json. The demo page shows them one at a time.

What Foundation needs

  • A behaviour checkpoint at the end of each of the four phases, with at least one question.
  • Any question can be used at any checkpoint, and questions do not need to get harder from phase to phase. Where you can, choose questions that fit what has just happened in the story. The table below shows which fit each phase.
  • The questions can be set in advance, or picked at random. Every player could get the same ones.
  • Each checkpoint takes no more than 2 minutes, so the game fits a facilitated session. A timer for the whole checkpoint, or 30 seconds a question, both work as long as the checkpoint stays within 2 minutes.
  • When time runs out, any unanswered question earns no points, the game still shows its explanation, and play moves on. A facilitator can pause the timer, for example while a group reads a long question aloud.
  • After each answer, the game shows the explanation.
  • A secure (correct) answer earns extra points. A wrong answer earns none. You decide how many points.
  • Show the behaviour points separately from the control score, then add the two together for the final outcome after phase 4.

An idea, if you want it: a harder question on the same topic

Start a checkpoint with an easier question. If the player answers it correctly, offer them a harder question on the same topic for more points, and so on up to tier 4. This is a design choice for your team, not a requirement. If you try it:

  • Tell the player the rule before the checkpoint starts.
  • A wrong answer still earns no points.
  • The whole checkpoint still takes no more than 2 minutes. Tier 4 questions take longer to read, so leave time for them.

This is different from the Reactive questions capability in Stretch. Here, the player's answers decide the next question. In Reactive questions, the controls the player chose decide which questions they get.

Rules for using the questions

  • Keep each question, its correct answer and its explanation exactly as written.
  • There is one correct answer. A wrong answer is never partly right.
  • You decide how many points a secure answer earns, and whether answers change anything else in the game. Tell the player what the rule is.
  • Keep the control score from the supplied scoring module visible on its own, so we can check it.

AI tools

You can use AI coding tools, and we expect most teams will. We judge your thinking, your design and your problem solving.

  • You own: how the system is structured, where the game's state lives, keeping the theme separate from the scoring, what gets tested, and being able to explain the code you hand in.
  • AI can help with: writing the code, interface components and styling, the tests you have designed, and the repetitive parts.
  • Your finished game must not call an online AI service while it runs.
  • Tell us in your hand-in note where AI tools helped.
